Mind the Interagent Gap

An evidence-graded index of multi-agent AI risks. Failure modes stand on their own evidence; risk factors are the mechanisms that can amplify them. Every piece of evidence reaches a risk through exactly one instance, so no claim is ever inferred from two tags co-occurring.

    Layer 1 · the ontology

    The risks, and how well each is grounded

    Three failure modes — what goes wrong — and seven risk factors — what makes it more likely. Grounding is the strongest single piece of evidence for that risk, 0–10 on the rubric, rescaled to 0–1. It is a maximum, so read it beside n: a 0.9 on two rows is not a well-studied risk.

    How risk factors relate to failure modes

    A failure mode is autonomous: it is grounded only by evidence of that failure occurring. A linked risk factor can amplify it — each ribbon is a link the authors assert, carrying that factor's own grounding to the failure mode. That routed figure is a mechanism the authors claim, not evidence that this factor produced this failure in any observed event.

    Layer 2 · instances, the link between evidence and risk

    The instance layer

    Each instance belongs to exactly one risk. Evidence attaches here, which is why a single event can never imply a relationship between two risks. Instances marked ours were authored because the inventory found behaviour the paper's Table 1 does not instantiate.

    How events map to instances

    Every piece of evidence: the instances assigned to it, how many, its tier, and the score breakdown across the five rubric criteria. Each criterion scores 0–2 and they sum to the 0–10 evidence score.

    Evidence tiers

    The model, and what follows from it

    • Instances are the link layer. Evidence attaches to an instance, and each instance belongs to exactly one risk. A row reaching both a failure mode and a risk factor does so through two separate instances — two independent claims, never evidence that the two are related.
    • Failure modes are autonomous. A failure mode's grounding comes only from evidence of that failure. Risk-factor grounding is routed into it along links the authors assert, and shown separately so the two are never added together.
    • Links come only from the authors. Either they argue it in prose, or one of their own Table 3 case studies sits under both ends. Links are never inferred from our own tags co-occurring.
    • Analogy references do not carry evidence. The paper cites the 2010 Flash Crash when discussing social dilemmas, military domains and markets. Table 3 does not tick those risks for it, so it does not ground them — otherwise a non-AI 2010 event would ground Conflict and Collusion at 0.9.
    • Inclusion test. An event is in scope only if the behaviour arises from interaction between two or more AI agents, one agent acting on another included.
    • Grounding is a maximum, not a sum. Corroboration is already priced inside each row as E4, so summing would let weak rows outrank one strong study. The cost: one strong row fully grounds a risk, so always read n alongside.
    • E1 is read mechanically off the tier (real world 2, synthetic 1, lab 0), so the score is not independent of the tier.
    • Real-world rows cannot earn E2 = 2. No controlled comparison exists for a live swarm, so observed events cap at 9 however well documented. Only ablations break that ceiling.
    • Collusion's real-world evidence is substantially one campaign. Several rows describe the same OpenAI events; whether they are one incident or several is an open decision.
    • The lab rows clustered at 3/10 share one unverified profile. None has been spot-checked for corroboration or venue, so treat those scores as a floor.
    • No coverage or validity figure appears anywhere on this page. The inventory of evaluation methods and the coverage mapping are not built. Nothing here says a risk is unmeasured — only that it is, or is not, evidenced.
